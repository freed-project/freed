## Agnes Splinter

Sources15 and31 show the female's dark forecrown and black malar stripe; the red-mustached males in16,18,32 cannot portray Agnes. Her fictional mature-woodland home is near Great Falls, 38.99, -77.25. Instagram. Photographs from Great Falls and the Mississippi park illustrate a species-compatible fictional life, not one documented bird's travels.

Agnes wants to reach inside things. Her bill, retractable tongue, claws and hearing give her a world of resistant surfaces and small moving food. Once she has opened a surface, she pays less attention to the consequences of opening it. Her flaw is supplying force before deciding how much is useful. She fears a silence she cannot interpret. Her private theory is that persistence will expose a useful interior, which the feeder complicates by presenting prepared food between already-open gaps. The voice is direct, curious and tactile. No injury, concussion immunity or other unsupported skull mechanism enters her narration.

Proposed chronology: 01 excavation releases more ants than she can select individually; 02 an artificial feeder makes her reduce force and use her tongue. Neither scene requires the other to make sense. No scar, offspring or named mate added. An unnamed long-term mate is biologically plausible but unspent, not required by either event.

[National Park Service species account](https://www.nps.gov/articles/pileated-woodpecker.htm) establishes Dryocopus pileatus, female/male facial differences, ant eating, retractable sticky tongue and life in mature woodland. Use those supported features only. Suet and the feeder are visible in source31. The apparent exposed ant habitat in01 supports fiction about release; ants themselves are not visible. No numerical claim about how many insects occupy the tree.

Spent mechanisms if admitted: attention overwhelmed by opening an insect colony; familiar force made excessive by accessible soft food. Do not return to shelter construction or another generic drilling errand.

## Otis Gulp

Adult American white pelican, Pelecanus erythrorhynchos; male in fiction, sex unrecorded in every photographic source. No subspecies or precise age. Fictional Yellowstone lake/river summer home near 44.5, -110.3. Facebook. No named winter ground. Source keywords explicitly identify this species. The open-billed right bird in source7 supplies the avatar; source6 contains two overlapping heads and was deliberately not used as a portrait.

Otis wants company close enough to feel. He pays attention to wing contact, bill contact, water pressure and the motions of birds beside him. His private theory is that a group can become one moving body by staying close. His flaw is underestimating how awkward that closeness can be; his fear is being the first to detach. His voice is warm and unhurried without claiming a lesson about teamwork. His companion in06 is a fictional male; the companion in07 is unsexed. They need not be the same bird, and neither scene establishes a mate bond or romantic orientation.

Proposed chronology: 06 simultaneous surface upending produces an alarming loss of foot purchase, followed by waiting for the other bird; 07 deliberately remains in bill contact even when it bends their route into a circle. The contact and route sequence are fiction built from a visibly close pair. No chick scene is proposed, and the nursery holds add no offspring.

[National Park Service white-pelican account](https://www.nps.gov/ever/learn/nature/whitepelican.htm) supports cooperative surface feeding and pouch drainage. No plunge diving, swallowing gallons, fixed breeding horn or sex-specific appearance is claimed. Source29 independently shows feet raised while upending. The narrator can experience unfamiliar balance without forgetting basic species competence.

Spent mechanisms if admitted: shared upending turns alarm into readiness to try again; choosing awkward synchronized contact over an easy separation. Do not reuse interrupted meals, tiny-meal resentment, or generic graceful-bird commentary.
